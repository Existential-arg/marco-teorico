The Existential Axis of Worth

The Existential Axis of Worth takes shape when, in the early years of life, a child develops a particular attention to recognition, achievement and the expression of their own value. This Axis is tied to discovering the ability to do, create, make an impact and be recognized for what one contributes.

Experience begins to organize itself around the motivation to deploy skills, reach goals and see that effort reflected in how others value it. An existential orientation develops that values growth, commitment, merit and the possibility of contributing in a meaningful way. Action becomes a legitimate path for personal expression and recognition.

Worth thus becomes an Axis that holds existence together: a way of being in life in which meaning is found in letting one's own potential shine and in contributing through what one is capable of achieving.

The Axis of Worth lived from Fear

The person feels they have no value in themselves, but must constantly prove it through their achievements, their effectiveness or their image. Outside recognition becomes essential to sustain their self-esteem, and their identity is built on the results they obtain or the impression they make on others.

From this experience, they may show:

  • A need to display achievements, efficiency or success in order to feel valuable.
  • Overadapting to what is expected, putting their own wishes or emotions on hold.
  • A tendency to define their identity by what they do rather than by who they are.
  • Fear of failure or of going unnoticed, which can lead to hyperactivity, anxiety or burnout.
  • Difficulty being with emptiness, rest or unproductive time, because they feel they lose value if they are not performing.

The person lives under constant pressure. Their energy goes into reaching visible goals, keeping up a positive image or exceeding expectations, with the fear that, if they fail, they will be exposed as someone without worth.

The Axis of Worth lived from Trust

The person knows they are valuable beyond what they achieve or show. There is an inner certainty that their worth does not depend on their performance but is inherent. From there they can act with commitment, pursue goals and develop their potential, without losing touch with their inner world or getting trapped in image or results.

From this experience, they show:

  • A deep sense of self-esteem, independent of success or outside validation.
  • The ability to act authentically, choosing what truly matters to them.
  • A genuine presence, with no need to impress or stand out all the time.
  • A drive to achieve, but out of desire and inner motivation, not out of pressure, comparison or competition.
  • The ability to value being beyond doing, recognizing their worth even in stillness or in error.

Worth is lived as something already present. The person can express it, share it and develop it, but does not need to prove it. This lets them act more freely, relate to others from what is real and keep a more human, grounded pace of life.

Childhood context

Exploring the childhood context allows us to understand more deeply how certain traits develop from an early age. Many of the behavior patterns we see in adults are rooted in childhood experiences and family dynamics. Understanding the context in which we first set the way we face our existence helps us understand how we face situations and challenges today.

Children who want to stand out

From an early age, a child with E-Mode 3 tends to show an active, ambitious, achievement-oriented nature. They are energetic and strongly motivated to stand out and succeed in all their activities.

  • In social situations they can be outgoing, sociable and good at adapting to different groups.
  • Their self-esteem is tied to achievement and outside recognition. They may feel especially satisfied and validated when praised for their abilities or successes, and constantly look for new ways to stand out.
  • They learned early on that love is replaced by awards, success and medals.
  • They may feel pressure to meet outside expectations and reach the standards of success set by authority figures.
  • In play and schoolwork they try to excel and be recognized, adjusting their behavior to win admiration.

Positive family context

  • Competence, achievement and recognition were valued, in balance with unconditional love and self-acceptance.
  • Achievements were celebrated as a natural part of growing up, not as a condition for being loved.
  • Strong emotional support, which made sure personal worth did not depend on performance alone.
  • Encouragement of authenticity: the genuine expression of feelings and thoughts.
  • Emphasis on collaboration and teamwork as part of personal and collective growth.

Message perceived in childhood: "You are loved for who you are."

Negative family context

  • The child may have felt ignored or invisible, or even looked down on for who they really were.
  • They tried to stand out to counter the fear of abandonment.
  • A lack of affection or recognition led them to overcompensate with achievements.
  • They took refuge behind a facade of success and disconnected from their authenticity.

Message perceived in childhood: "It's not OK to have your own feelings and identity."

Perspective · ThinkFrom Fear

What do they think?

From the fear of being seen as inadequate or as a failure, they may have negative or limiting thoughts focused on valuing themselves through achievement and success. Some examples of the inner narrative:

  • Success orientation: "I have to be successful and efficient in everything I do to be valued and respected."
  • Fear of failure: "Failure isn't an option; it would be devastating for my self-esteem and my public image."
  • Worth through achievement: "People will only value me if I have successes and achievements to show."
  • Constant productivity: "I always need to be doing something productive or working toward a goal."
  • Image of success: "It's crucial that others see me as competent and successful."
  • Comparison: "I have to surpass, or at least match, other people's success to keep my position."
  • Denying emotions: "There's no time for emotions or weaknesses; they could get in the way of my performance."
  • Adapting to win: "I can and must adapt to whatever it takes to get to the top."
  • Self-promotion: "If I don't promote myself, no one will recognize my talents and skills."
  • Identifying with success: "My identity is tied to my successes and achievements."

These thoughts can keep them in a constant state of activity and pursuit of achievement, often at the expense of their relationships and emotional wellbeing. The inner pressure to maintain an image of success can disconnect them from their own feelings and authentic needs, and from those of others.

Affective · FeelFrom Fear

What do they feel?

From the fear of being considered a failure or not enough, they may experience emotions that they try to manage or even hide in order to keep their image of success and effectiveness:

  • Anxiety: about the possibility of not meeting their own or others' expectations and, therefore, not being admired or valued.
  • Frustration: at obstacles that keep them from reaching their goals and sustaining their image of success.
  • Shame: when their efforts do not translate into the expected achievements.
  • Fear of rejection: constant worry about how they are perceived and whether they will be accepted and valued.
  • Envy: when comparing themselves with others who seem to have more success or recognition.
  • Anger: when they feel others do not appreciate their effort or underestimate them.
  • Impatience: from the need to get results quickly and move on to the next achievement.

These emotions can be especially intense in moments of high pressure or when they sense their status or image is at risk. To sustain their self-image they may repress them or not attend to them, which sometimes disconnects them from their true feelings and wishes.

Effective · DoFrom Fear

What do they do?

From Fear, they are driven by an intense need to be seen as successful and to avoid failure at all costs. They may show these behaviors:

  • Competitiveness: they strive to be the best at what they do, and often turn non-competitive situations into contests.
  • Overidentifying with success: their self-esteem is closely tied to their achievements and to how others perceive them.
  • Adaptability: they may change their image or attitude to fit what they believe others value.
  • Overworking: constant activity and hard work to reach their goals and secure their status.
  • Neglecting self-care: they often sacrifice their health and personal wellbeing for work and success.
  • Lack of authenticity: they may lose touch with what they really want or feel, and prioritize what they believe will make them more valuable in others' eyes.
  • Avoiding emotions: they tend to repress or ignore their feelings to stay focused on the task.
  • Personal marketing: they promote themselves and their successes, sometimes exaggerating their achievements to impress.
  • Rationalizing failure: when facing setbacks, they may minimize or rationalize them so as not to feel affected.
  • Impression management: they may manipulate how others see them, showing only their most favorable sides and hiding their flaws and vulnerabilities.

Perspective · ThinkFrom Trust

What do they think?

When they live their Axis from Trust, their thoughts reflect a more authentic outlook, aligned with their true identity:

  • Self-acceptance: "I'm fine just as I am, beyond my achievements."
  • Inherent worth: "My value doesn't depend on my performance or on the image others have of me."
  • Authenticity: "It matters to me to be genuine and true to myself and to others."
  • Personal growth: "Personal growth is worth more than simply piling up successes."
  • Relationships: "Meaningful relationships are as important as my professional achievements."
  • Balance: "It's important to find a balance between work and experiences shared just for the joy of sharing."
  • Empathy: "Understanding other people's feelings and connecting with them enriches my life."
  • Satisfaction in the process: "Enjoying the process is as satisfying as reaching the goal."
  • Long-term perspective: "Failures are opportunities for learning and growth."
  • Personal purpose: "I want to find and follow my own path, not just what's expected of me."

These thoughts steer them toward a more integrated life, in which they stay aligned with their true identity and values instead of seeking only outside success and validation.

Affective · FeelFrom Trust

What do they feel?

They experience emotions that reflect more authenticity and connection with their inner life:

  • Genuine joy: a happiness that comes from accepting themselves and from meaningful connections with others, not the fleeting joy of successes and achievements.
  • Satisfaction: a sense of fulfillment with life that does not depend solely on their achievements or on outside recognition.
  • Gratitude: for experiences, for the people in their life and for their own and others' achievements, valuing the process as much as the result.
  • Compassion: a deeper compassion for others, which connects them with their struggles and successes.
  • Calm: peace from not having to seek approval all the time or try to impress.
  • Confidence: in their intrinsic worth, beyond their achievements or public image.
  • Courage: to show themselves as they are and pursue what truly matters to them, even if that means taking risks or facing failure.
  • Self-love: a healthy self-love that lets them take care of themselves and set healthy boundaries.
  • Hope: a positive outlook on the future, based on a balanced, realistic view of their abilities and possibilities.

They experience a rich and balanced emotional life, which fosters meaningful relationships and a sense of authenticity and purpose in what they do.

Effective · DoFrom Trust

What do they do?

Their actions reflect a more authentic connection with their true wishes and values, and greater consideration for others:

  • Authenticity: they act as who they are, not as who they think they should be to impress or win approval.
  • Balance: they find a healthy balance between work and "unproductive" activities, and recognize the importance of rest and quality time with loved ones.
  • Team spirit: they collaborate and promote shared success, instead of competing all the time to stand out individually.
  • Humility: they recognize and accept their flaws and mistakes, without feeling that these affect their worth as a person.
  • Empathy: they show more sensitivity to other people's feelings and needs, instead of focusing only on their own goals.
  • Self-acceptance: they accept themselves with their limitations and do not base their self-esteem solely on their achievements or the image they project.
  • Growth: they focus on their personal growth and on developing their potential in a healthy way, rather than on piling up successes to impress.
  • Contribution: they look for ways to contribute to the wellbeing of others and of the world, and put their talents at the service of ends more meaningful than personal recognition.
  • Vulnerability: they dare to be vulnerable and share what they really think and feel, which lets them build deeper bonds.

Efficiency and success are balanced with sincerity and human connection.

From Fear to Trust

The integration process of E-Mode 3 begins when they stop acting, feeling and thinking from fear, actively working on their fears and exploring new possibilities, and start doing so from trust. In this way they enrich the way they face existence and begin to show positive traits of E-Mode 6 (loyalty, reliability and the ability to anticipate and address problems), which they usually did not develop or nurture enough. They move toward authenticity, self-confidence and the ability to face challenges with resilience, trusting their inner compass.

Integration is, therefore, the process through which a wider range of competencies, skills and resources is acquired to build a fuller, more authentic existence.

Perspective · Think

Perspective: from Fear to Trust

Shifts in thinking that support their integration may include:

  • From outer success to inner worth: from "My worth comes from my achievements" to "I'm valuable for who I am, not only for what I do."
  • From image to authenticity: from "I have to keep up an image of success" to "It's OK to show myself as I am, with strengths and weaknesses."
  • From doing to being: from "My life always has to be full of activity" to "I can find value in stillness and in simply being."
  • From competition to collaboration: from "I have to be the best" to "I can collaborate and celebrate other people's success."
  • From efficiency to emotions: from "Feelings have to be efficient" to "My emotions matter and deserve attention."
  • From recognition to self-acceptance: from "I need others' recognition to feel good" to "I recognize and accept myself."
  • From adapting to personal values: from "I adapt to what others consider successful" to "I stay true to my own values and beliefs."
  • From speed to depth: from "Everything has to be done fast" to "Taking time for depth and reflection is valuable."
  • From productivity to personal health: from "I always have to be producing" to "My personal wellbeing is a priority."
  • From self-promotion to genuine contribution: from "I have to promote myself to get ahead" to "I contribute meaningfully without always needing to be the center of attention."
  • From what others will say to critical thinking: from "I lean toward other people's opinions and expectations" to "I question and assess information and others' opinions independently."

Fostering these thoughts helps them focus less on success as the definition of their identity and more on authenticity and on personal growth, their own and that of others.

Affective · Feel

Affective: from Fear to Trust

To work with their emotions in favor of integration, they can try:

  • Emotional recognition: taking time to identify and acknowledge their real emotions, instead of the ones they think they should have.
  • Honest expression: learning to express their emotions openly and authentically, instead of suppressing or disguising them to protect their image.
  • Emotional presence: practicing mindfulness with their emotions, observing them without judgment to better understand their reactions.
  • Self-acceptance: accepting that it is human to feel the whole range of emotions, including vulnerable ones, and that they do not diminish their worth or their success.
  • Emotional journal: recording their emotional experiences to process and understand them more deeply.
  • Slowing down: giving themselves permission to pause and reflect on how they really feel, especially before making important decisions.
  • Self-care: regular activities that nurture their emotional wellbeing, such as exercise, meditation, therapy or hobbies.
  • Emotional connection with others: building relationships where vulnerability is welcome and they can share their emotions without fear of judgment or criticism.
  • Allowing themselves to be imperfect: freeing themselves from the need to be seen as perfect and allowing themselves to feel and show emotions that may be perceived as imperfect or weak.

By working on these areas they can live more authentically and with greater emotional richness, and connect with their insecurities and fears, which makes them more loyal, authentic and courageous.

Effective · Do

Effective: from Fear to Trust

Behaviors that can help:

  • Practicing authenticity: making sure their actions and words faithfully reflect what they really think and feel.
  • Seeking genuine feedback: asking for and appreciating constructive feedback, not just praise.
  • Prioritizing relationships: devoting time to deepening personal bonds beyond professional networks.
  • Fostering more authentic relationships: valuing bonds based on authenticity, trust and loyalty.
  • Team mindset: valuing cooperation and group work to reach common goals.
  • Resilience in adversity: facing and overcoming challenges with more resilience and determination, drawing on their ability to anticipate and solve problems. Being able to stick with a bet they consider important, despite failures or a lack of payoff for their image.
  • Valuing the process: enjoying the road toward achievements, not just the end result.
  • Resting and reflecting: taking conscious breaks for personal reflection, which improve their connection with what they do, their focus and their long-term productivity.
  • Personal goals: setting goals that reflect their values, not only goals aimed at outside recognition.
  • Balancing achievement and leisure: dividing their time between working to achieve and activities that bring them pleasure, relaxation and engagement with others.
  • Attending to their needs: caring for their physical, emotional and spiritual wellbeing without feeling it is a detour from their goals.
  • Acting selflessly: taking part in activities that benefit others without expecting reward or recognition.

With these behaviors they align their actions with who they really are, find more satisfaction in life and build deeper, more meaningful relationships.

Levels of integration

DisintegratedSemi-integratedIntegrated

Disintegrated

Desperation and deception. They fear failure and may begin to deceive themselves and others to sustain their image. They may resort to manipulation and opportunism to cover up their emotional deterioration. At the lowest level they can become ruthless, with a great deal of anger and hostility, and even want revenge on those they see as threats to their image or status.

  • Effective: manipulative and opportunistic; aggressively competitive.
  • Affective: anger and tension, inner and outer; an intense fear of failure.
  • Perspective: obsessive thoughts about outside recognition and the achievements still to be reached.

Semi-integrated

Success orientation and concern with image. They begin to fear that others will outdo them, so they strive to stand out and achieve more to draw attention. They worry about keeping up an impressive image; they are ambitious but insecure inside. They may promote themselves excessively and show competitiveness and arrogance to hide their need for recognition.

  • Effective: they push hard and focus on standing out.
  • Affective: tension and anxiety about achievement.
  • Perspective: a distorted self-perception; they overrate themselves.

Integrated

Authenticity and self-acceptance. They free themselves from the belief that their worth depends on others' opinions, which lets them discover their true identity and follow their heart's desires. They feel valuable and useful in themselves, accept themselves and are genuine. They adapt well, are goal-oriented and progress in their own development: they are competent, secure and an example for others.

  • Effective: they accept themselves and are genuine; oriented toward goals and their own development.
  • Affective: they feel valuable and useful, connected with their true feelings and wishes.
  • Perspective: they free themselves from the belief that their worth depends on others' opinions.

C-Modes: C2 and C4

C-Modes (complementary modes) influence and nuance the characteristics of each person's E-Mode, creating greater variety and richness in behavior. They are the E-Modes on either side of one's own: if someone has E-Mode 9, their C-Modes are 8 and 1.

Understanding C-Modes helps explain the range of behaviors that people with the same E-Mode can show. An E-Mode may show positive and negative traits of one or both C-Modes, only the negative ones, only the positive ones, or a combination.

Affective triad

The three triads group the E-Modes according to the dimension in which their way of facing existence is mainly anchored: the effective (action), the affective (emotion) and the perspective (thought).

E-Mode 3 belongs to the affective triad: it covers the emotional qualities that allow us to relate to other people and feel connected to them. It has to do with emotions, empathy and self-image. E-Mode 3 belongs to this triad because of its underlying motivation, deeply tied to being valued and admired by others: that need for outside validation is rooted in their emotions, particularly in their sense of personal worth. They seek recognition and success as a way to feel valuable and loved, which implies a complex connection with their feelings, even if indirect or unconscious.

  • Focus: their own and others' emotions; relationships and personal identity.
  • Seeks: attention and relationship.
  • Identity and hostility: deep down they know their identity does not express who they really are, and they react with hostility when it is not valued, to divert the attention of those who might question or underestimate it.

Challenges

  • They demand a lot of themselves and of others in order to succeed, and those demands turn into aggression.
  • They direct their attention and energy both inward and outward: they need reactions, positive opinions and validation from others. They seek worth mainly through achievement, form an idea of what a valuable person would be like and try to be that person. They risk believing their "own propaganda" more than the truth about themselves.
  • Concern with image: they may be overly focused on their image and on how others perceive them.
  • Difficulty identifying their real feelings: at times they find it hard to recognize and express what they truly feel, because they are focused on success.
  • Excessive competitiveness: their wish to be the best can lead to unhealthy competitiveness.

Contribution

  • Success orientation and efficiency: very focused on reaching their goals, efficient and competent in what they do.
  • Adaptability and charm: a great ability to adapt to different situations and people; often charming and charismatic.
  • Motivation and energy: highly motivated and energetic, which helps them reach high levels of achievement.
